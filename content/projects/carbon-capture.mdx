Carbon capture is one of the most important and most underfunded areas of climate tech. Most people treat it as a backup plan, something we'll need if emissions cuts fail. The more I researched, the more I became convinced it's a requirement, not a backup.

## The focus

The project ran from October 2023 through summer 2024. It focused on direct air capture (DAC): pulling CO₂ straight from the atmosphere instead of from point sources like power plants.

DAC costs more and uses more energy than point-source capture. But it's the only approach that draws down past emissions.

## The question

What are the real ways to bring the cost down? Today DAC costs $400 to $1,000 per ton of CO₂, depending on the system. The most promising levers:

- Learning curve analysis
- Materials innovation
- Process heat integration

## Policy and markets

I also looked at carbon credit markets, government incentives like the 45Q tax credit, and how different regulations change the economics.

The research sharpened my thinking on how hard, long-term problems get solved, and how rarely the path looks like anyone predicted.

---

**Outcome:** Produced an independent research report on direct air capture (DAC), covering cost reduction pathways, materials innovation, and the policy and market dynamics shaping the space.

**What I took from it:**
- Carbon capture isn't a backup plan. The emissions math doesn't close without it.
- The real levers for cost are learning curves, materials, and process heat, not just subsidies. Knowing *why* something is expensive tells you where to push.
- Hard, long-term problems rarely get solved the way anyone predicted. I got more comfortable with that uncertainty instead of needing the path to be clear before starting.
