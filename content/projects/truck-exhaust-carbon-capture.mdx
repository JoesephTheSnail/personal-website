In Grade 9 I drew a carbon capture unit that bolts onto the back of a truck. It pulls the CO₂ out of the exhaust before it leaves the tailpipe. I thought it was a great idea, but I never checked whether it would actually help.

So I went back and worked it out properly. The exhaust runs through a liquid called amine, which soaks up the CO₂. Heat the amine to about 90 to 100°C and it lets the CO₂ go, so the same amine can be used again. The heat comes from the exhaust itself. The CO₂ gets stored in tanks on the truck, and a small bin of spare amine replaces what wears out.

The tricky part is energy. The system needs power to run, so how much CO₂ it catches isn't the real number. What matters is what's left after you take that away. That's why I added a unit that turns some of the exhaust heat back into electricity.

**Why it matters.** Diesel trucks are some of the hardest vehicles to switch to electric, and they put out CO₂ the whole time they drive. Catching it at the tailpipe could cut emissions from trucks that will be on the road for a long time yet. But it only works if the system removes more CO₂ than it costs to run, which is the question I kept coming back to.

A drawing can't show how all of this fits together, so I built the truck in 3D with React Three Fiber. Spin it, zoom in, and tap any part to see what it does.

---

**Outcome:** Turned a Grade 9 idea into a worked-out system for capturing CO₂ from truck exhaust, plus a 3D model that shows how each part works.

**What I took from it:**
- How much CO₂ a system captures sounds impressive. What's left after its own energy use is the number that counts, and it's a lot smaller.
- Going back to an old idea means finding out what you got wrong. Most of my fixes were to assumptions, not to the drawing.
- If you can't explain something, build it. Making the 3D model showed me parts of my layout that didn't fit together.
